# Who decides? A permitted auditor choice sets the outcome of one in five NYC AI bias audits

Matthew Jackson · independent researcher, Detroit · 2026-10-25 · working note, not peer reviewed · published in the preprint *Four-Fifths of What?*, doi:10.5281/zenodo.23672859

## Claim

New York City's rule for AI hiring-tool bias audits (Local Law 144; 6 RCNY 5-301(d)) lets the auditor, who is hired by the company being audited, choose whether to drop any demographic group under 2% of applicants from the impact-ratio calculation. Both choices are lawful.

Across all 114 scorable audits in the ACLU *Auditing the Audits* corpus, that one choice alone decides whether the audit clears the four-fifths line in **22 audits (19.3%)**, from 14 employers and 8 audit firms.

- Under the point reading the audits invite, the option **can only help the audited company**. This is structural, not an observed pattern: under the four-fifths point reading, dropping a group can only lower the reference rate or remove a ratio, so it can turn a fail into a pass but never the reverse. All 22 are fail-to-pass by construction.
- **19 of the 22** published ratios that show the passing version. Of six of those reports retrieved on 2026-10-26, four could be read: three say in their own words that categories under 2% were excluded (one adds that the excluded groups would otherwise have set the highest rate), and the fourth removed its highest-rate group under a 300-applicant minimum the auditor set itself.
- **Not driven by the largest audit firm.** One firm supplies most of the corpus and appears to use reference rates pooled across deployments. Without its audits, the option decides **16 of the remaining 44 audits (36%)**, across 11 employers, and 15 of the 16 published the passing version.
- The groups dropped are mostly real groups: of the 34 highest-rate groups the option removes from the reference position (each counted once per audit), **19 have 100 or more applicants and 5 have over 1,000** (median 149; largest 26,214). The 2% cut is relative to the pool, so large pools can drop large groups. **28 of the 34 are American Indian or Alaska Native, or Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander groups**: under the point reading, the option that can only help the company works mostly by removing Native groups from the comparison.

The headline numbers are exact and regenerated by code (`discretion.py`); tests pin them (`test_mandates.py`).

## Why it matters

LL144 is one of the first laws anywhere to require published bias audits of AI hiring tools. The finding shows a concrete mechanism of self-regulation inside it: a parameter that decides outcomes is set by the regulated party's contractor, not by the public or the regulator, and in practice it is set in the company's favour. Parameter choices like this exist in every bias-audit regime, and few are this visible.

## Method

1. Corpus: ACLU, *Auditing the Audits* (CC BY-NC 4.0), `audits/audits_full.csv`, SHA-256 `69fd290f…bf85e`. 116 audits, 114 with usable counts. Groups recorded as Missing/Unknown excluded, as the rule requires.
2. Statistics: [libra-check 0.2.0](https://github.com/MattJaxson/libra-check), LARS v0.1.
3. Each audit judged under two machine-readable rulebooks that encode the two lawful options (`examples/ll144-as-permitted-exclude.json`, `examples/ll144-as-permitted-include.json`): threshold 0.80, conventional point reading, identical in every respect except the exclusion.
4. "As published" uses the impact ratios printed in each audit.

## Robustness

Reading every ratio through its 90% or 95% interval instead of the bare number (which also guards against tiny, noisy reference groups), the option still changes the outcome of **21 to 30 audits**, favouring the company in 14–27 of them versus 3–7 against.

| Reading | Outcome depends on option | Option favours company | Option against company |
|---|---|---|---|
| 90% intervals | 30 | 27 | 3 |
| 90% intervals, all comparisons together | 23 | 16 | 7 |
| 95% intervals | 23 | 20 | 3 |
| 95% intervals, all comparisons together | 21 | 14 | 7 |

**Tiered tools.** Some tools report two selection definitions ("top" and "top and middle"); the headline judges both together. Judging each audit on one definition gives **20 ("top") to 35 ("top and middle")** audits, and 14–16 of the 44 audits outside the largest firm's reports. The option never turns a pass into a fail under any of them.

**Reconstructed counts.** Most published counts are rebuilt from rounded rates. Allowing every count consistent with the published rates, the option still decides **17 of the 22** whatever the true counts; in the other 5, some consistent counts would make the audit fail even with the option exercised.

## What this does not show

- It does not show that any hiring tool discriminates, or that any employer broke the law. LL144 requires publishing an audit, not passing one, and sets no pass mark. "Fails" means the four-fifths reading of the published counts.
- It does not show that any auditor chose exclusion to help a client. "Published the passing version" is consistent with exclusion having been applied; motive is not observed.
- Excluding a very small reference group can be statistically reasonable. The point is that the rule leaves it to the auditor's discretion, and the interval readings above show the dependence survives once noise is accounted for.
- The corpus is the audits the ACLU could collect, and one audit firm produced about 72% of the ratios; the result is reported with and without that firm. Repeat audits of the same employer are not independent, so the employer count (14, or 11 without that firm) is the more conservative unit.
- Interval readings are the empirical test of direction: there the option goes against the company in 3–7 audits, so the one-way property is specific to the point reading the law invites.

## The infrastructure response: community mandates

The fix is not a better auditor. It is moving the choice. This repository defines a **Community Mandate** (`mandate.schema.json`): a machine-readable rulebook in which a public body sets every parameter that decides an outcome, including threshold, uncertainty reading, exclusion, who carries the burden when evidence is unsettled, and whether test data counts. Each mandate records its authority and process, an adopted mandate must cite a public record of the decision, and every mandate has a content hash, so an evaluation is bound to the exact rules used.

`engine.py` judges any published audit under any mandate. Same evidence, the public's rules. Under the illustrative community mandate (an example adopted by no one), the 22 audits above are not certified: 21 are "insufficient evidence" and 1 fails.

Relation to prior work: statistical critiques of the four-fifths rule at small samples go back decades (UGESP 1978; Morris & Lobsenz 2000). Wright et al. (FAccT 2024, *Null Compliance*) found that 20% of impact ratios in the audits they examined were omitted under the 2% rule, mostly for Indigenous groups, and Gerchick et al. (FAccT 2025, *Auditing the Audits*) note the same pattern for NHPI and AIAN applicants. Black, Gillis & Hall (FAccT 2024, *D-hacking*) name the general risk of choosing among defensible analyses. As far as the author has found (search of 2026-10-26; Google Scholar full text, SSRN and law reviews not yet searched), none measures how often the exclusion decides published audit outcomes, which is what this note does.
